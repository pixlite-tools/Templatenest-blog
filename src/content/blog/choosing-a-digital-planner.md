Digital planners have a rough survival rate. Someone buys a beautifully designed one, uses it for four days, and quietly goes back to a blank notes app. The problem is rarely the planner's design — it's that most people buy based on how a planner *looks* in the preview, not how it fits the way they actually plan.

Here's a more reliable way to choose.

## Start with your planning unit, not the layout

Before looking at any template, answer one question: do you think in **days**, **weeks**, or **projects**?

- If you're the type who checks off small tasks constantly, a **daily** layout with hour blocks will feel supportive, not restrictive.
- If your work happens in bigger, less linear chunks, a **weekly** spread with flexible task lists will breathe better than a rigid hourly grid.
- If most of your life is organized around multi-week initiatives, look for planners with a **project or goal-tracking layer**, not just a calendar grid.

A gorgeous daily planner will fail you if you actually think in weeks — you'll spend more time renegotiating the format than using it.

## Digital-first, not print-scanned-to-digital

Some "digital planners" are just PDF scans of a print layout, dropped into a PDF annotation app. They technically work, but the tap targets are tiny and navigation is clunky. Look specifically for planners built **for** GoodNotes, Notability, or a tablet interface — with linked tabs, clickable month/week navigation, and hyperlinked pages. This is usually mentioned explicitly in the product description; if it isn't, assume it's a scanned print layout.

## Check for a habit or reflection section — but only if you'll use it

Habit trackers and gratitude logs are the most commonly *ignored* section of any planner. If you know you won't fill in a mood tracker, don't let a planner's inclusion of one be a deciding factor either way. Judge the sections you'll actually touch daily: the task list, the calendar, the notes area.

## Undated vs. dated

Dated planners (with real calendar dates baked in) are easier to use if you're consistent — everything lines up automatically. Undated planners are more forgiving if you fall off for a week; you just pick the next blank page instead of staring at three skipped days as a monument to failure. If you've abandoned planners before due to guilt over gaps, undated is very likely the better fit.

## Test the file before committing to the system

Most sellers include a free sample page or a short preview video — actually open it on the device and app you'll use daily. Tap through the navigation. If it feels clumsy in the first thirty seconds, it will feel clumsy every morning after that.

## The honest bar for success

A planner "working" doesn't mean using every section perfectly for a year. It means opening it most days without friction. If a layout gets you to open the app more often than you did before, it's doing its job — regardless of how many features you never touch.
