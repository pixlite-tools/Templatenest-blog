If you freelance, you already know the real work isn't the design, the writing, or the code — it's the fifteen small systems running underneath it. Client intake. Invoicing. Deadlines. Where-did-I-put-that-file. Notion is good at holding all of that in one place, but only if the template you start from matches how you actually work, not how a productivity influencer works.

Here are the templates worth having, organized by the problem they solve.

## 1. A single client hub

Every client should have one page: contact details, scope, rates, key dates, and a running log of decisions. The mistake most people make is spreading this across email threads and sticky notes. One database, filtered by status (Active, Proposal, Past), solves 80% of the "wait, what did we agree on?" problem.

## 2. A project tracker with real statuses

Not just "To Do / Doing / Done." You want statuses that match freelance reality: *Awaiting Brief*, *In Progress*, *Client Review*, *Revisions*, *Invoiced*, *Paid*. When a project can sit in "Client Review" for two weeks, your tracker should be able to say so at a glance.

## 3. An invoice and payment log

This is the one people skip and regret. A simple table — client, amount, date sent, date paid, days outstanding — turns "I think that invoice is overdue" into a five-second lookup, and makes tax season dramatically less painful.

## 4. A content or deliverables calendar

If you produce anything with a publish date — posts, designs, episodes — a calendar view tied to your project database keeps deadlines visible without needing a separate tool.

## 5. A rate and scope calculator

A lightweight internal page where you can plug in hours, complexity, and rush fees, and get a suggested quote. It won't replace judgment, but it stops the "I'll just guess" pricing spiral.

## 6. A weekly review template

Freelancing has no manager checking in on you, so the check-in has to be self-imposed. A short weekly review — what shipped, what's overdue, what needs following up — takes ten minutes and prevents most fires before they start.

## 7. A proposal and contract tracker

Separate from your active client hub: a table of every proposal sent, its status, and follow-up dates. Most freelance income leaks out of unfollowed-up proposals, not underpricing.

## 8. An asset and file index

Not the files themselves — just an index of where everything lives (Drive folder, Figma file, GitHub repo) per client. Five minutes of setup saves twenty minutes of searching, every single time you reopen an old project.

## 9. A recurring-task checklist

Things you do for every client, every time: kickoff email, contract sent, files delivered, invoice sent. A simple recurring checklist template keeps you from relying on memory for repeatable steps.

## 10. A "someday" idea vault

For services you might offer later, or process improvements you don't have time for now. Getting them out of your head and into a page you'll revisit quarterly keeps your active workspace focused on what's actually in motion.

## The real point

None of these templates are complicated. The value isn't the database structure — it's not having to *design* the structure yourself while you're also trying to do client work. A good template is a decision you only have to make once.
