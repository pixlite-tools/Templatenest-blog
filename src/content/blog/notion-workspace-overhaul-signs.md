Notion workspaces don't collapse all at once. They erode slowly — one new database here, one abandoned page there — until six months later you're spending more time searching for things than using them. If any of the following sound familiar, it's not a "you" problem. It's a structure problem, and it's fixable.

## 1. You have more than one place a task could "live"

If a new to-do could reasonably go into your Tasks database, a project page's checklist, or a random "Inbox" page you made three months ago and forgot about — you no longer have a task system, you have three half-systems competing for the same job. The fix isn't more discipline; it's collapsing them into one database with views, not separate homes.

## 2. You've made a "master" page linking to your other pages

This one feels productive in the moment — a homepage with links to everything — but it's usually a sign that your workspace has no real information architecture underneath it. A well-structured workspace doesn't need a manually maintained directory; navigation happens through databases, filters, and a sidebar that mirrors how you actually think.

## 3. You avoid opening Notion for certain tasks

If you've caught yourself tracking something in Apple Notes, a spreadsheet, or a sticky note "just for now" instead of adding it to Notion — pay attention to what that task was. It's usually a sign that the corresponding part of your workspace has too much friction: too many required fields, a confusing view, or a database that was built for a use case you've since outgrown.

## 4. Your properties don't match how you filter

Open one of your core databases and look at the properties. If half of them are never used in a filter, sort, or view — they're not helping you, they're just data entry tax you pay on every new item. A good template earns every property by using it somewhere visible.

## 5. You've renamed the same database twice

Renaming a database is often a quiet admission that its purpose has shifted since you built it, but the underlying structure hasn't caught up. If "Content Calendar" became "Content + Client Work" became "Everything," the database is now serving three jobs with one shape — and that mismatch is exactly what makes it feel clunky to use.

## What a rebuild actually looks like

An overhaul doesn't mean starting from zero. It means:

1. Listing every *type* of thing you track (tasks, projects, clients, content, notes).
2. Assigning each type exactly one database — no duplicates, no "temporary" second homes.
3. Building views (not new pages) for the different ways you need to see that data — by status, by date, by owner.
4. Migrating your existing entries into the new structure in one sitting, rather than gradually, so you're not maintaining two systems at once.

A structural rebuild done once, deliberately, beats six months of small patches that never quite fix the underlying drift.
